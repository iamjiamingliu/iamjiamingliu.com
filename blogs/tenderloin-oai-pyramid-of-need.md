## The Context

In my previous article [it's all there](/blogs/its-all-there.md),
I claimed that,
there's rarely completely new knowledge,
mostly just new combination of them to applied new contexts,
which make them super-interesting nevertheless.
Much knowledge is already in the books or internet,
but it's one thing to be aware of something,
it's another to actually understand something,
it's totally something else to be able to experience something related,
and actually connecting the experience to the abstract concept model.

So, here's one out of many, many examples I've been thinking about recently:
pyramid of needs.

Why I've been thinking about it?

As a summer intern on ChatGPT,
many of the peers are very ambitious, talented, and accomplished for our age,
and a super frequent conversation them is: what are you doing next?
Are you chasing to become an AI researcher or joining a quantitative trading firm to make the big bucks (cash or paper money)?
Are you trying to stay in the current role as an engineer and try to have a bigger impact and leadereship roles?
And then what? See [The Rat Race](/blogs/rat-race.md) for full context.

So, what do we _really need_ next?
And then I thought about Maslov's psychological definition of Pyramid of Needs.
Well, that helped answer some uncertainty in my thinking, at least it provided a structure to my thinking.
And I realized how my summer interning at OpenAI is able to touch some of the lowest parts of the pyramid of needs
all the way up to the abstract parts.

Along the way, I will also throw in some examples from my high school and college days in general.

## My Summer and The Pyramid of Need

This is the iconic diagram for pyramid of needs:

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/60/Maslow%27s_Hierarchy_of_Needs.svg/1920px-Maslow%27s_Hierarchy_of_Needs.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=imageinfo&utm_content=thumbnail)

And Wikipedia adds that, "In his later writings, Maslow added a sixth level of "meta-needs" and meta-motivation".

By definition of a pyramid,
whatever is at a lower level would appear to the boring and unnoticed,
but,
when they are damanged or worse if removed completely,
upper layers of the pyramid would crumble and that's when you start to appreciate a cornerstone's importance.

My summer somehow illustrated this pyramid pretty thoroughly, so, that's a curious case study.

### Physiological

Breathing, homeostasis, and excretion-wise,
thankfully,
my summer has been fine.
Usually these are only damaged when one's sick.
which I haven't bumped into this summer yet but certainly bumped into it every quarter at UCSB
(people get sick in college so. so. frequently. omg.),
and I've seen friends getting sick for various reasons and symptoms this summer or in life in general.

And yeah I agree.
When one is healthy,
you wouldn't think about it at all.
You only notice it when it goes wrong.
When you are 100 Fahrenheit in bed swallowing Tylenol's,
the only two things you would pray for are "please make all sickness go away and why does disease exist in the first place"
and "oh gosh I'm gonna miss that school / work deadline and I'm fucked for return offer or bonus or promotion."

There's a reason people say "prioritize health first" because if you don't you just die and nothing else happens.

Food and water wise,
I realized just how brittle I am (more like, how every human is).
If it's a work day and I'm at the office, I'm saved by free cafeteria food and drinks.
But if it's at night at home or on the weekend, I start to appreciate and understand the importance of water and food security as basic needs.
If I got lost scrolling or playing video games on the weekend and eat some hours late,
my body just start shivering and I actually feel my activity is slowing down;
but thankfully all it takes to realive me is a hamberger and then I'm up and running again.

For water, I'm basically dependent on my friend and another fellow intern and neighbor Sam Wang's britta filter bottle;
whenever he's not home, I start to panic a bit and have to go on an Odyssey across the tenderloin street to buy plastic bottled water half a mile away.

Sleep-wise, oh wow, I should write an essay on this by itself.

For me,
if I don't sleep more than 8 hours,
even 30 minutes less,
I would just wake up and be a zombie for the rest of the day.
My head would think at reduced capacity,
and I would get very moody,
and all sorts of things that happen in the higher levels of pyramid of need just won't happen because the cornerstone of sleep is missing.
When I sleep for any minute more than 8 hours though,
holy,
I really do see the world as sunshine rainbow and lolipop even though there's 6 or 7 things burning down that day.
This meme describes me on happy days pretty well:

![](https://s.yimg.com/lo/mysterio/api/88663b4cd37205acd2bbb6a9f43e2456d6805491aff7afb99e01a7d2b44c65cf/lightyear_networkapi/resizefill_w485_h477%3Bquality_80%3Bformat_webp/https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.zenfs.com%2Fen%2Fknow_your_meme_909%2Fb5aceee2712d9c0c50378634ea3d16df)

I believe gen z is at a sleep deprivation crisis.
So many college students around my age are living with 5-6 hours of sleep per day and that's just horrible.
I see so many people, across well-accomplished and not well accomplished alike,
sleeping at 3am on some days and 6am other days and waking up feeling shit couple hours later.
Every news outlet is talking about AI, finance, entertainment, and other stuff,
and there's mental health on the headline too.
But I'm somewhat confident (but don't quote me clinically on this) that if every zen Z in this world is blessed with 8 hours of sleep,
likely many can be happier; at least it's a net positive.

It's a privilege to have good sleep.
I have a few friends with chronic sleep problems.
Their eyes always seem half open and their words sound feeble,
and I feel sad for them.
Imagine how much cooler and happier and productive and amazing they can be if they can also sleep 8 hours a day.

### Safety

I am living in SF Tenderloin district this summer,
notorious for its scary streets with drug abuse and high density of crimes.
Oh boy do I have some stuff to say about safety in one's hierarchy of needs.

After I get my burger and water from Sam Wang and that 8 hours of sleep on good days,
I open the door and I know,
"here's another Odyssey across tenderloin street I guess."

Foul smell of human feces around the valleys and streets,
actual human feces on the streets,
shabby homeless individuals shouting at passer-bys or at each other
-- safety is not just the physical baseline of not being harmed,
but more frequently the psychological perception that you can live through another day.
Police and ambulance sirens every half an hour...
oh boy, center of the AI revolution they said.

I knew SF Tenderloin would be bad, but I didn't know it would be this bad.
Every night I get out of the Waymo, I just hurry up and zoom past the foul street and into the apartment door
while scanning my head around the environment like how those Lidars on Waymo spin around.

One time some interns hosted a birthday party,
we got back to the apartment at 11:30pm;
15 minutes later as I was able to take a shower,
I heard 10 loud pongs on the plaza outside the apartment building,
then I heard crowd (just a few people) screaming and scattering and shouting "take cover and stay down",
then I heard police and ambulance rushing towards my apartment, rescuing a few guys on the sketchers and shutting down the scene with yellow warning day,
and the next day on major news:

![](https://cdn.abcotvs.com/dip/images/19581208_072626-kgo-market-st-shooting-img.jpg?w=1024)

**ABC News: 4 injured in shooting after trying to break up argument in SF Tenderloin, police say.**

Hell nah. The worst way to end one's life is dying in a summer internship because you lived in Tenderloin.
Whenever I go back home to Cupertino the suburb,
I just say,
wow,
what a quiet and safe and peaceful place to be that I just took for granted.
I'm never living in the city again.
At least not in Tenderloin spinning my head like Waymo lidars everyday.

By Wikipedia,
"safety" in pyramid of needs goes beyond physical safety to job safety and financial safety too.
In the age of AI,
no one -- not even people within the AI companies -- concur whether AI will completely outclass humans or greatly supplement us.
Things are moving fast,
predictions back when Cursor came out like "software engineers will be replaced by 2025" didn't happenm,
but it doesn't mean it won't happen a few years from now.

I guess one theory I am willing to accept and cope with the most is,
if AI at most supplements us, then we are fine;
otherwise if they outclass some of us,
it likely will outclass us bottom up in terms of expertise and talent,
so that just raises the bar for a good-enough human to get a job.
And there's also the possibility of AI unlocking new human opportunities, so stay tuned for that as well.

Finally in terms of financial safety,
I'm doing fine this summer.
I eat company food on the week days and go out friends on the weekends,
so I am able to save up.

Fuck the rent though. It's $2,000 per person per month.

I overcome an Odyssey across the Tenderloin street to work,
come back with a paycheck that Uncle Sam take a share first,
and then the landlord takes another hit?
All the hours stayed at the office just for the landlord?

### Love and Belonging

Thankfully,
I live with 3 other interns in Tenderloin,
and we get along nicely.
On the weekends we would grab food and travel to nearby places.
And at work, there's also many who I can smile and wave and chat a little bit too, so that's nice.

And when I go back to Cupertino on some weekends,
perfect,
I have even more folks to hold on to.
These days thanks to social media,
I can also always send reels and memes and funny stuff over Instagram and Discord and Wechat.

It's nice to have friends.
Throughout my time as a student from Kindergarten to high school,
I'm always the new guy who joined late.
I started Kindergarten 2 years later than everyone else because I would get sick too often and my mom and dad thought I wouldn't make it,
so when I started Kindergarten,
I just ate quietly and didn't have many folks I call as friends.
Elementary school and middle school were alright,
but once I got to the U.S.,
at least for the first few weeks,
things are a little rough.
After all,
people have known each other from elementary school,
to middle school,
and then high school;
their parents know each other,
their friends are each other's friends,
everything overlap already,
and it's hard to blend in as a newcomer.

Thankfully,
I was still able to find my own little circle of friends.
In middle school, it was a bunch of international Chinese homies that we go to each others house, go to volunteering events, and do silly stuff together.
Since high school, my friends had been from tech bros I met via clubs and various projects we did together.
You don't realize the value of friends until all of a sudden,
you are dropped into the middle of nowhere,
and you go home every night tired and empty on the weekends realizing,
dang,
I have nothing to do.
This is what happened to me in [summer 2024](/blogs/jiaming-at-bytedance.md) when I interned in China in a City with no friends or family.
So yeah, it's nice to have friends.

It's nice to feel like you belong, because I clearly have an example of what happens when you don't.
I joined Cupertino ASB (student government) back in my high school junior year - it was not a welcoming place at first.
Due to the popular kids effect - that is, whoever is in student government is usually the clique of popular kids -
I didn't fit in. I knew nobody when I first joined ASB.
And, whenever I tried to talk to someone else about getting a task done, a new idea proposal,
or even a friendly small talk,
it seems as if their time is more valuable than a YC partner's investor pitch.
Their head just turns away.
And, oh boy, at such a young age, they already mastered corporate golden phrases from
"I will get back to you on this" (of course, they never) to "I agree, but..." (it means fuck you, hell no).
Yeah, it's not nice to not feel welcomed.
Feeling welcome is never about the explicit things,
it's always about the small things that subconsciously add up to a conclusion of "you are not welcome here."
And when it comes to feelings, subconsciously is usually the truth, because truth is by definition subjective when it comes to feelings which is subjective.
So, I thought about quitting.
I was doing meaningfully work.
I was making an impact.
But I didn't feel welcome.
And that's all it takes to make me want to quit.
Fortunately, I just grinded my teeth through the first semester.
And in the second semester,
I was able to pick up my solo journey to found Tino News Today (the school TV newscast) program,
so I distanced myself from the unwelcome hell with an adventure, and that certainly helped.
If you don't feel welcomed, just distance yourself away - well, that's an unfortunate solution.
The next year, I was able to meet a few like-minded peers in ASB, and I was no longer lonely, and I stayed for the rest of my high school.

Every aspect of pyramid of need I talked about so far mostly relates to one's self
-- not being attacked on the street, getting enough water and sleep, not going broke, etc --
and starting from here and levels of the pyramid above,
it's not just a solo problem anymore,
but a social problem.
Belonging, confidence, morality, creativity -- these are mostly determined by social acceptances more than an individual's passive state.
